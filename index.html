Tippecanoe County — Upzoning Opportunity

What this map shows

Each shape is an ownership site — one or more adjacent parcels held by the same owner, merged into a single development footprint. Sites are coloured by land assessed value per square foot: darker red means the market values that land more highly per unit area.

This is a proxy for where density would be most economically viable if zoning allowed it. Locations where land is expensive relative to its area are the places developers would most want to build — and where upzoning would most likely trigger new construction.

How land AV is calculated

Indiana law requires all property to be assessed at 100% of market value-in-use. The Tippecanoe County Assessor uses a Computer-Assisted Mass Appraisal (CAMA) system that splits each parcel's total assessed value into two parts:

  • Land AV — the value of the land as if it were vacant, derived from comparable vacant-land sales and location-based pricing by neighbourhood.
  • Improvement AV — the depreciated replacement cost of whatever is built on it.

The county submits annual ratio studies — comparing assessed values to actual sale prices — to the Indiana Department of Local Government Finance (DLGF) for certification.

Limitations

  • The land/improvement split is not independently validated. Ratio studies only check total assessed value against sale price. The split between land AV and improvement AV is generated by CAMA model assumptions and is the least reliable part of the assessment — particularly for vacant and underutilised parcels.
  • The score measures demand, not constraint. A high land $/sqft means the location is valuable; it does not mean current zoning is what's holding back development. Other factors — financing, construction costs, ownership fragmentation — may be equally binding.
  • Assessment data lags the market. Residential values are trended annually against sales; commercial values use cost tables updated every four years. Fast-moving markets may be under-assessed.
  • Owner name matching is imperfect. Ownership sites are assembled by normalising and grouping owner names from county records. Entities with inconsistent naming may appear as separate sites.
